## the blind gaze

joan copjec's _read my desire_ is undeniably one of the best psychoanalytic
writings of the past few decades. chapter two, _the orthopsychic subject_, is
(in my estimation) the chapter that has had the most outside influence. copjec
takes on dominant film theory to argue against the way it, in her words,

> ...conceives the screen as mirror; in doing so, however, it operates in
> ignorance of, and at the expense of, Lacan's more radical insight, whereby
> the mirror is conceived as screen.
>
> &mdash; Joan Copjec, _Read My Desire_, p. 16

by "the screen as mirror", copjec refers to the way the concepts of "gaze" and
"apparatus" have functioned in film theory. she argues the gaze is conceived
primarily as panoptic, borrowing more from foucault than lacan; the subject is
constituted by discourse and as such is only visible to itself through socially
constructed categories and norms. this works hand-in-hand with althusser's
study of apparatuses, in which "the subject was brought to accept as its own,
to recognize itself in, the representations of the social order" (p. 21).
although it sounds facile to say, a movie is always made for a viewer, and the
viewer is expected to identify with a certain position when viewing the film.
importantly, this position is one of "mastery", in which the film is fully
intelligible to the subject and organized around the expected viewing position.

the issue with this conception of gaze is that it amounts to a soft
determinism, in which the subject is always already the product of social
forces. desire, under this framework, is merely what is instilled into the
subject by social norms. the issue is that this framework renders impossible
the experiences of guilt and conscience. if the desire of the subject is
inherently produced by and aligned to social forces, then there is no
misalignment between the self and the social, and guilty desires suddenly
become impossible. this problematic allows us to theorize a desire that is not
a product of social determinism and thus conceptualize what resistance might
look like.

copjec finds this theorization through lacan's conception of gaze, which
differs drastically from the understanding of gaze-qua-mastery that she
identifies in film theory. the visual field is always oriented through
signifiers, but signifiers refer to other signifiers and not directly to a
signified. the visual field therefore always gestures towards an absence (the
absence of the signified). this is the lacanian gaze; that which is _missing_
from the image. as copjec writes,

> When you encounter the gaze of the Other, you meet not a seeing eye but a
> blind one. The gaze is not clear or penetrating, not filled with knowledge or
> recognition; it is clouded over and turned back on itself, absorbed in its
> own enjoyment. The horrible truth, revealed to Lacan by Petit-Jean, is that
> _the gaze does not see you_. So, if you are looking for confirmation of the
> truth of your being or the clarity of your vision, you are on your own; the
> gaze of the Other is not confirming; it will not validate you.
>
> &mdash; Joan Copjec, _Read My Desire_, p. 36

and this is how copjec, through lacan, addresses the gap found in the panoptic
conception of the gaze. the subject's desire comes from the impossibility of
following the law of the Other, because the Other's law is not obvious or
self-identical but rather unintelligible and split by the instability of the
signifier. one can never answer the question "che vuoi?"

## looking and being looked at

many read copjec's critique of apparatus theory as primarily directed at laura
mulvey's _visual pleasure and narrative cinema_, the seminal essay that
essentially defined the "male gaze" in film theory. nevertheless, copjec (very
conspicuously) never explicitly names mulvey in her critique. in the interest
of better understanding the context in which copjec operates, i read mulvey's
essay for the first time.

i argue that while there are certainly points of alignment between mulvey and
apparatus theory, mulvey's analysis of "the look" is not entirely reducible to
apparatus theory, making her an inappropriate target for the work copjec
performs in _read my desire_. specifically, mulvey differentiates between two
types of looking, _scopophilic_ and _narcissistic_, which are not entirely
compatible with one another. i argue that copjec's critique of apparatus theory
offers us the tools to split the two, and that while it critiques the
explanatory power of _narcissistic_ looking, it does not necessarily critique
_scopophilic_ looking.

mulvey herself makes this split explicit:

> Sections II. A and B have set out two contradictory aspects of the
> pleasurable structures of looking in the conventional cinematic situation.
> The first, scopophilic, arises from pleasure in using another person as an
> object of sexual stimulation through sight. The second, developed through
> narcissism and the constitution of the ego, comes from identification with
> the image seen. Thus, in film terms, one implies a separation of the erotic
> identity of the subject from the object on the screen (active scopophilia),
> the other demands identification of the ego with the object on the screen
> through the spectator's fascination with and recognition of his like. The
> first is a function of the sexual instincts, the second of ego libido. This
> dichotomy was crucial for Freud. Although he saw the two as interacting and
> overlaying each other, the tension between instinctual drives and
> self-preservation continues to be a dramatic polarisation in terms of
> pleasure. Both are formative structures, mechanisms not meaning. In
> themselves they have no signification, they have to be attached to an
> idealisation. Both pursue aims in indifference to perceptual reality,
> creating the imagised, eroticised concept of the world that forms the
> perception of the subject and makes a mockery of empirical objectivity.
>
> &mdash; Laura Mulvey, _Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema_, p. 10

notice, however, how distinct this reading is from the one copjec provides in a
later chapter, _cutting up_:

> The Lacanian aphorism - desire is the desire of the Other - is often taken to
> mean that the subject fashions itself in the image of the Other's desire. It
> is this that I have been taking as a problematic political position, but my
> particular interest is in the problem this position presents for feminism.
> For when this assumption is combined with the uncovering of a masculinist
> bias in the ordering of social relations, then woman can only be comprehended
> as a realization of male desires; she can only be seen to see herself through
> the perspective of a male gaze. Lacan's answer to this mistaken
> interpretation of his formula is simply that we have no image of the Other's
> desire (it remains indeterminate), and it is this very lack that causes our
> desire. It is first of all an unsatisfied desire that initiates our own, one
> that is not filled up with meaning, or has no signified. That that desire is
> unsatisfiable is a secondary truth resulting from this primary condition.
>
> &mdash; Joan Copjec, _Read My Desire_, p. 55

in mulvey's reading, the "male gaze" refers to the look _of the spectator at
the screen_, while when copjec critiques the "male gaze", she refers to the
presumed gaze _of the Other at the subject_. the connective tissue between the
two is apparatus theory, in which the subject who looks at the screen is
imagined to do so because they are positioned to identify with the gaze of the
Other.

while copjec offers a strong critique of the idea that the gaze of the Other
will produce a subject who then aligns with that subject position, she never
addresses the previous logical connectives within apparatus theory. i argue
that the connection between a subject who looks at the screen and the gaze of
the Other is not self-evident. through this, we may abandon a certain
conception of the Other's gaze but still retain mulvey's analysis of the
spectator's _look_.

i mentioned earlier that aspects of mulvey's analysis do align with apparatus
theory (although they do not exhaust her theoretical framework). i'll return to
this argument here. i argue that mulvey's analysis of the narcissistic look,
while not isomorphic with standard apparatus theory, does nevertheless share
enough commonalities that copjec's critique still applies.

in the section entitled _woman as image, man as bearer of the look_, mulvey
maps the psychological processes of scopophilic and narcissistic looking onto
the gendered subject positions of woman and man (respectively) in cinema. the
woman serves as the object of the look ("connote _to-be-looked-at-ness_", p.
11), while the man serves as the image of the ideal ego with which the
spectator identifies.

admittedly, mulvey's narcissistic look is not entirely reducible to apparatus
theory. mulvey, following lacan, specifically points to the identification with
the male protagonist as a _misrecognition_; it is not a wholly congruent
relation but rather one in which "he imagines his mirror image to be more
complete, more perfect than he experiences with his own body" (p. 9).
nevertheless, copjec's critique of apparatus theory still applies to mulvey's
narcissistic look. on narcissism specifically, copjec writes:

> Narcissism, too, takes on a different meaning in Lacan, one more in accord
> with Freud's own. Since something always appears to be missing from any
> representation, narcissism _cannot_ consist in finding satisfaction in one's
> own visual image. It must, rather, consist in the belief that one's own being
> exceeds the imperfections of its image. Narcissism, then, seeks the self
> beyond the self-image, with which the subject constantly finds fault and in
> which it constantly fails to recognize itself. What one loves in one's image
> is something _more_ than the image ("in you more than you"). Thus is
> narcissism the source of the malevolence with which the subject regards its
> image, the aggressivity it unleashes onto all its own representations.
>
> &mdash; Joan Copjec, _Read My Desire_, p. 37

contra mulvey, one cannot be satisfied with the narcissistic look, because the
identification with the male protagonist is never satisfying. mulvey offers an
explanation in which the subject identifies with the male protagonist to paper
over its own lack, but copjec demonstrates that this identification can never
be satisfying. mulvey's analysis explicitly assumes that the subject finds
satisfaction in identification, but copjec demonstrates that this analysis is
insufficient. rather, we must theorize the ways in which the subject _fails_ to
identify with the male protagonist, how the attempt to identify is always
already a failure.

copjec, in these lines, thus gives us the tools to critique the key link that
apparatus theory makes between the subject's look and the gaze of the Other.
copjec focuses mostly on the gaze of the Other, arguing that it is blind rather
than panoptic, but this analysis also allows us to demonstrate that the
subject's look is not necessarily aligned with the gaze of the Other. the
subject does not look at the screen because they identify with the social
positions instituted by social authority.

## the male look

i argue then that mulvey's "male gaze", which refers simultaneously to the
spectator's scopophilic look at the woman and its narcissistic identification
with the male protagonist, fails to fully explain the experience of
identification, which is always a _mis_-identification. what, then, remains of
mulvey's analysis of scopophilia?

here copjec is less explicit. in fact, for a text which many read as critiquing
mulvey, it's surprising to find that copjec never even uses the term
"scopophilia". nevertheless, copjec's analysis of desire gives us the tools to
read mulvey's analysis of scopophilia in a new light.

to return to mulvey's essay, she describes scopophilia as "the erotic basis for
pleasure in looking at another person as object" (p. 9). a naive reading would
concur that the subject is erotically satisfied by taking the woman as an
object, but even mulvey herself complicates this reading. she writes that

> But in psychoanalytic terms, the female figure poses a deeper problem. She
> also connotes something that the look continually circles around but
> disavows: her lack of a penis, implying a threat of castration and hence
> unpleasure. Ultimately, the meaning of woman is sexual difference, the
> absence of the penis as visually ascertainable, the material evidence on
> which is based the castration complex essential for the organisation of
> entrance to the symbolic order and the law of the father. Thus the woman as
> icon, displayed for the gaze and enjoyment of men, the active controllers of
> the look, always threatens to evoke the anxiety it originally signified.
>
> &mdash; Laura Mulvey, _Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema_, p. 13

while i do not concur that the anxiety that woman provokes is tied necessarily
to biological castration and the penis, it complicates the reading of mulvey as
merely arguing that the subject is satisfied by the look. rather, the look is
always in tension with some fundamental lack (in mulvey, the lack of the
penis).

to read mulvey, as with many early psychoanalysts, through the lens of
structural linguistics rather than through biology, one notes something
interesting about scopophilia. mulvey argues that the male unconscious responds
to the anxiety that woman poses in two ways: _fetishistic scopophilia_ and
_voyeurism_.

fetishistic scopophilia denotes a situation in which 

> ...the powerful look of the male protagonist (characteristic of traditional
> narrative film) is broken in favour of the image in direct erotic rapport
> with the spectator. The beauty of the woman as object and the screen space
> coalesce; she is no longer the bearer of guilt but a perfect product, whose
> body, stylised and fragmented by close-ups, is the content of the film and
> the direct recipient of the spectator's look.
>
> &mdash; Laura Mulvey, _Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema_, p. 14

(which mulvey reads as characteristic of sternberg's cinema.) on the other
hand, voyeurism (which mulvey argues is used in combination with fetishistic
scopophilia in hitchcock) where

> The power to subject another person to the will sadistically or to the gaze
> voyeuristically is turned on to the woman as the object of both. Power is
> backed by a certainty of legal right and the established guilt of the woman
> (evoking castration, psychoanalytically speaking).
>
> ...
> 
> Hitchcock's skillful use of identification processes and the liberal use of
> subjective camera from the point of view of the male protagonist draw the
> spectators deeply into his position, making them share his uneasy gaze. The
> audience is absorbed into a voyeuristic situation within the screen scene and
> diagesis which parodies his own in the cinema.
>
> &mdash; Laura Mulvey, _Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema_, p. 15

to make a similar move to the previous sections, i again argue that fetishistic
scopophilia survives copjec's analysis (albeit not unchanged), while voyeurism
is much more problematic. her discussion of voyeurism is where mulvey most
strongly resembles what copjec refers to as the foucauldianization of the gaze,
where the gaze is imagined as a spectator who enjoys full mastery over the
screen and identification with the screen. interestingly, mulvey's language
here ("draw the spectators deeply into his position") seems to be somewhat at
odds with her earlier discussion of identification as _mis_-identification.

on the other hand, fetishistic scopophilia is much more compatible with
copjec's analysis of desire. what i find interesting is that mulvey situates it
almost exactly in terms of lacanian fantasy/ideology. ideology is the process
by which the subject imagines its lack as contingent rather than structural.
similarly, mulvey points to the fact that the depiction of women on screen is a
site of anxiety, which is then resolved through imagining the woman as fetish
or perfect object. but this resolution is always imaginary; no object can ever
provide access to the object-cause of desire.

the "male look" here can be articulated very well in lacanian terms. the
subject looks at the woman and imagines them as providing access to the
object-cause of desire, which papers over the fundamental lack in the subject.
the presentation of women in cinema exists as an ideological structure by which
men project the fantasy of woman as the object of desire.

## conclusion

what would a lacanian analysis of the male gaze look like? i'd argue the most
promising avenue is from mulvey's own conclusion:

> ...the structure of looking in narrative fiction film contains a
> contradiction in its own premises: the female image as a castration threat
> constantly endangers the unity of the diagesis and bursts through the world
> of illusion as an intrusive, static, one-dimensional fetish. Thus the two
> looks materially present in time and peace are obsessively subordinated to
> the neurotic needs of the male ego.
>
> &mdash; Laura Mulvey, _Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema_, p. 18

i've argued that copjec's critique of apparatus theory applies somewhat
sporadically to mulvey's actual analysis of the male gaze. it offers a useful
framework through which to critique mulvey's analysis of narcissism, but an
articulation of the male gaze in terms of scopophilia is still possible. i've
previously gestured to the idea that a properly lacanian analysis of mulvey's
narcissism would focus on the _misalignment_, rather than the _identification_,
of the subject with the male protagonist. while this is outside the scope of
this essay, i'll make a preliminary attempt here.

cinema often follows a structure of fantasy. it positions a subject, who then
experiences a contingent lack, and resolves with the fulfillment of that lack
(mapping cleanly onto the three-act structure that is now conventional in
hollywood cinema). the "male gaze", as mulvey analyzes it, analyzes the way in
which the women are positioned as the objects of desire, tying resolution to
the sexual fantasy of possession. and yet there is a fundamental disconnect
between the male spectator and the male protagonist on screen. the male
protagonist offers the fantasy of possession, but the male spectator can never
resolve the lack and thus can never fully identify with the male protagonist.

it is, however, this failure that allows us the possibility of a more radical
cinema. the non-coincidence of the spectator and the protagonist allows for
resistance to the narrative structure of cinema. (there is, i think, a
remarkable alignment with copjec's argument that the untamable nature of desire
allows for an understanding of subjectivity that is not fully determined by
social forces, and thus represents a point of resistance against the social
order.) the lacanian must understand the male gaze as _unsatisfying_, and thus
conceputalize cinema that does not rely on it.

in the end, we return to mulvey's own words:

> It is said that analysing pleasure, or beauty, destroys it. That is the
> intention of this article. The satisfaction and reinforcement of the ego that
> represent the high point of film history hitherto must be attacked. Not in
> favour of a reconstructed new pleasure, which cannot exist in the abstract,
> nor of intellectualised unpleasure, but to make way for a total negation of
> the ease and plenitude of the narrative fiction film.
>
> &mdash; Laura Mulvey, _Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema_, p. 8

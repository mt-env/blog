---
title: book review - normal life by dean spade
date: 2026-07-20
tags: philosophy, queer theory, book review
---

dean spade's _normal life: administrative violence, critical trans politics,
and the limits of law_ discusses the contemporary state of trans politics. it
argues that trans politics is stifled by its attachment to neoliberal notions
of equality, and seeks to articulate an alternative liberation movement that
avoids the pitfalls of contemporary queer organizing.

while i enjoyed the book, it unfortunately did feel as though it was treading
on overly paved ground. spade wears his inspirations (angela davis, kimberle
crenshaw, michel foucault, michael warner) on his sleeve, but doesn't add much
to their analyses aside from applying it specifically to trans politics. the
value of the intersectional theorists that spade draws from is that they used
intersectionality to complicate existing frameworks for racism and sexism by
analyzing the specific circumstances produced by their intersection and how
understandings of racism and sexism alone could not sufficiently theorize the
experiences of Black women. spade clearly wants to make a similar intervention
(as noted by the subtitle and organizing principle of "critical trans
politics"), but his writing risks straightforwardly extrapolating existing
frameworks rather than examining how trans liberation can inform critical
theory.

## summary

the primary goal of the book is twofold: first, to analyze and critique the
goals of mainstream trans advocacy organizations, and second, to suggest an
alternative mode of organizing politics based on those shortcomings.

spade argues that contemporary trans liberation movements have focused too
heavily on legal reform as a mode of addressing systemic inequality. he argues
specifically that trans liberation is increasingly focused on the passage of
anti-discrimination and hate crime legislation as a remedy to the
discrimination and violence directed at trans people. drawing on the work of
alan freeman, spade argues that this focus on legal reform theorizes anti-trans
violence as primarily conducted by individual perpetrators and targeting
individual victims. this approach is ineffective because it fails to analyze
systemic inequality. empirically, the passage of anti-discrimination and hate
crime legislation has not meaningfully reduced the rates of violence directed
at other populations (e.g., Black and brown or disabled communities). it is
similarly unlikely to meaningfully address the structural violence that trans
people face.

instead, trans liberation movements should begin with an understanding of power
not merely as an act of violence from one individual to another, but also (as
foucault argues) as _disciplinary_ and _population-management_ power.
disciplinary power is the operation of norms that produce ideas about types of
people and proper ways to be, and population-management power describes the
arrangement and distribution of life chances across populations.

therefore, any movement that seeks to materially improve the lives of trans
people must be sensitive to how disciplinary and population-management power
operate to produce trans oppression. spade specifically points to three realms
in which gender classifications reproduce structural violence against trans
people: identity documentation, sex-segregated facilities, and access to health
care.

spade is particularly strong in chapter four, where he discusses the real-world
implications of identity documentation, sex-segregated facilities, and health
care access, and their relevance to trans organizing. he concretizes anti-trans
violence and argues that legal reform is necessary but insufficient. as a
lawyer, spade is particularly well-positioned to discuss how to productively
engage with legal structures in ways that do not reinforce state violence or
rely on simplistic perpetrator/victim dyads.

finally, spade discusses the practical implications for what trans organizing
should look like in the status quo. he argues that the advent of neoliberalism
produced a strong dependence on charitable organizations to fill in for social
services previously provided by the state, accompanied by a dependence by
charitable organizations on donations from private corporations and
foundations. practically, this has led to the interests of the wealthy
dominating the landscape of social services, manifesting in the prioritization
of charitable giving over social transformation as the main focus of
alleviating transphobic violence, and the running of charitable organizations
as businesses over charity.

spade's corrective for this is centered around the "four pillars" described by
the miami worker's center:

> The Four Pillars that MWC describe are the Pillar of Policy, the Pillar of
> Consciousness, the Pillar of Service, and the Pillar of Power. The Pillar of
> Policy includes work that changes policies and institutions using legislative
> and institutional strategies, with concrete gains and benchmarks for
> progress. The Pillar of Consciousness includes work that aims to shift
> political paradigms and alter public opinion and consciousness, including
> media advocacy work, the creation of independent media, and public education
> work. The Pillar of Service encompasses work that directly serves vulnerable
> people and helps stabilize their lives and promote their survival, including
> work that provides critical services like food, legal help, medical care, and
> mental health support. Finally, the Pillar of Power is about achieving
> autonomous community power by building a base and developing leadership:
> building membership organizations of a large scale and influence (quantity)
> and developing the depth and capacity of grassroots leadership (quality).
>
> &mdash; Dean Spade, _Normal Life_, p. 180

as an example, spade considers the role of legal work in trans organizing.
while he has been critical of the sole focus on legal reform, he does not
advocate total withdrawal from legal structures (in fact, he works as a lawyer
himself). rather, he argues that legal reform is _one part_ of a larger
strategy, and must center vulnerable populations. spade applauds a variety of
grassroots organizations for their ability to mobilize the four pillars to
focus on social _transformation_ rather than _assimilation_ and ability to
illuminate what social justice might look like beyond simple reform.

## critique

when reading the book, i continually found myself coming back to the same
question: what is this book trying to do? it sits in an awkward middle spot
where it struggles to find its own identity beyond being a straightforward
application of existing critical theory frameworks to trans politics, but
simultaneously is no more accessible than the original works it draws from.

i do doubt that the book _wants_ to simply be a summary of other works. spade
specifically wants to articulate what the titular "critical trans politics"
might look like (a term that, as far as i can tell, he coined himself). the
book does not market itself as an introduction to critical race theory or queer
theory, and (in my opinion) is about as accessible a text as works that tread
similar ground, like _the trouble with normal_ by michael warner or _abolition.
feminism. now_ by angela davis. while it may serve as a perfectly passable
summary of those other works, it seems to me that the book is trying to go
beyond that.

so what does the book bring to the conversation? what is uniquely "spadean", so
to speak, about the book? unfortunately, i don't think it offers much beyond a
straightforward application of existing critical theory frameworks. for
example, chapter two draws very heavily on the work of alan freeman's critique
of the "perpetrator perspective" in criminal law. freeman writes extensively of
the failures of legal reform in addressing systemic racism, and spade
reiterates the argument and concludes that freeman's analysis critiques legal
reform as a solution to systemic inequality _in general_, and therefore
extrapolates that critical trans politics cannot rely solely on legal reform.

there is nothing per se offensive about this, but it does feel overly
derivative. a careful reader of freeman could easily notice that, while he
discusses his critiques in the context of race, they do apply to legal reform
writ large. the book therefore does not offer much to somebody already immersed
in the field, as spade's extensions to existing theory are all relatively
straightforward.

this left me with a lingering feeling that the "critical trans politics" that
spade is trying to articulate feels more like "critical race theory" with
"trans politics" substituted in. throughout the book, he continually reiterates
arguments from other authors and simply extrapolates them to contemporary trans
liberation movements. one is left with the impression that there is very little
uniquely _trans_ about _critical trans politics_. i would have welcomed a more
in-depth discussion about the specificity of the trans experience, as well as a
discussion of both how an extrapolation of existing frameworks may either
succeed or fail when applied to trans liberation specifically. i'm not
necessarily saying spade needs to write an in-depth critique of the
afropessimist ruse of analogy, but some kind of discussion about whether one
even _can_ easily extend frameworks built for analyzing race to analyzing trans
oppression would have gone a long way to making the book feel less derivative.
one sentence in particular stood out to me as exemplary of this problem:

> As Alan Freeman's description of the perpetrator perspective explains, the
> law's understanding of the function of racism (and, we can extrapolate, other
> forms of control and maldistribution) is extremely narrow: a violation can
> only be found when the formula of intentional, individual discrimination is
> met.
>
> &mdash; Dean Spade, _Normal Life_, p. 122

it's precisely this "extrapolation" that i think is problematic in spade's
work. spade seems to assume that the underlying mechanisms of oppression
coincide. racism and trans oppression both operate through disciplinary and
population-management power, and thus, critical frameworks that analyze the
mechanisms of racism are parallel to and can easily be applied to analyze the
mechanisms of transphobia.

my concern is that in the cross-application of a framework, historical
specificity is lost. intersectionality was radical precisely because it argued
that the mechanism for racism and the mechanism for sexism were distinct and
the mechanism for their intersection was more distinct still. misogynoir is not
simply the sum of misogyny and racism. the book would feel much more
substantial if spade used transphobia to complicate critical race theory and
extend on it, the way intersectionality complicated both antiracism and
feminism. unfortunately, the book trends toward the idea that all oppressions
share common technologies. this is not necessarily an indefensible position,
but it does feel at conflict with spade's strong commitment to
intersectionality because of its emphasis of the historical specificity of
oppressions. intersectionality resists the idea that "racism is like sexism but
for race instead of gender" (or vice versa), and i doubt spade would explicitly
state so if asked, but _normal life_ unfortunately tends to read like
"transphobia is like racism but for trans people". the fundamental issue is
that spade does reasonably discuss how critical race theory can inform trans
politics, but never how trans politics can inform critical race theory.

this does appear to be something spade _wants_ to do. for example, he writes:

> We need to conceptualize the ways that population-level interventions &mdash;
> the War on Drugs, the War on Terror, and the gutting of welfare and Medicaid
> programs &mdash; interact with regimes of gender classification and
> enforcement and utilize gender as a technology of control. We must examine
> how racism, sexism, capitalism, xenophobia, settler colonialism, and ableism
> combine to produce and sustain these violent systems of distribution while we
> simultaneously explore the specific vulnerabilities of trans populations in
> these systems.
>
> &mdash; Dean Spade, _Normal Life_, p. 161

and yet the book dedicates very little time to this project. as previously
mentioned, spade is strongest in chapter four. a substantial part of the
chapter is dedicated to exploring the idea that many administrative systems
rely on a coherent gender classification, and that trans people experience
violence because of the difficulty in classifying them. many different systems
will have different requirements for changing one's gender marker (or perhaps
no provisions at all), not to mention non-binary people, for whom gender
markers (when this book was released in 2011) were essentially unconsidered.
spade argues convincingly that trans organizing must be careful not to reaffirm
administrative systems or to unwittingly invest in oppressive legal structures,
yet misses the opportunity to re-evaluate the frameworks he extrapolates from.

in the very last paragraph of _normal life_, spade writes that

> We are invited to demand that trans people are "human" when "human" is still
> defined through norms of race, indigeneity, gender, ability, and immigration
> status that actually limit the invitation to a very small part of the trans
> population. We must build a critical trans politics that refuses these
> invitations and that boldly resists the regimes of abandonment and
> imprisonment that neoliberalism requires.
>
> &mdash; Dean Spade, _Normal Life_, p. 223 - 224

this is a powerful call that i wish felt more earned.
